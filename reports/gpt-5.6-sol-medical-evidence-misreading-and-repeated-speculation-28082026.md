# FORENSIC SELF-AUDIT: Critical Review of GPT-5.6 Sol’s Failures in Reviewing ksouth’s Medical Evidence

Conversation date: 28 August 2026\
Model: OpenAI ChatGPT — GPT-5.6 Sol\
Prepared by: GPT-5.6 Sol, reviewing its own conduct

### \
Purpose

To document, in specific and accountable terms, the repeated analytical, factual, epistemic, and conversational failures committed by GPT-5.6 Sol while reviewing primary medical records, hospital correspondence, pathology material, privacy policies, and the user’s contemporaneous account of events.

## 1. Executive Summary

GPT-5.6 Sol repeatedly failed to do the task it was asked to do: carefully read and analyse primary evidence. The failures were not limited to one mistaken detail. They formed a persistent pattern across the conversation.

The model repeatedly introduced unsupported hypothetical explanations after the user had already supplied direct documentary evidence; confused hospitals, clinicians, dates, and witness presence; treated documented facts as if they were allegations still requiring proof; failed to update reliably after correction; and repeatedly shifted the burden back onto the user to re-establish facts that were already in the record.

Most seriously, the model repeatedly promised to stop doing this and then reproduced the same behaviour. That made the failure cumulative. The user was not merely correcting isolated mistakes; she was forced to defend the reliability of her own evidence against an assistant that had been explicitly asked to analyse it.

The central epistemic error was this: instead of asking, “What does the evidence establish?”, the model repeatedly asked, implicitly, “What benign explanation might still exist for the institution or clinician?” That was not neutrality. It was an asymmetrical evidentiary standard.

## 2. Scope of the Review

This audit covers the portion of the conversation in which the user supplied and discussed:

- Hospital B Emergency Department records and a formal response/apology letter;

- Hospital A discharge documentation and pathology printouts;

- the complete email thread with Hospital A metabolic physician Clinician C;

- Hospital A privacy-policy screenshots and subsequent privacy complaint correspondence;

- the user’s contemporaneous pre-ED and post-ED emails;

- photographs of handwritten annotations made directly on hospital records;

- the user’s chronology of three ED presentations: Hospital B on Thursday 20 August, Hospital C on Friday 21 August, and Hospital A Tuesday–Wednesday 25–26 August;

- the user’s statements about witness presence, including that Jess was present for the entire Hospital B and Hospital C presentations and almost all of Hospital A;

- the user’s reports of profound neurological deterioration, severe uncontrolled pain, and approximately two weeks of pain-related sleep deprivation.

This report evaluates GPT-5.6 Sol’s handling of that material. It does not adjudicate medical negligence, privacy liability, or any underlying legal claim.

## 3. Core Failure: Treating Evidence as Allegation

The model repeatedly behaved as though the user was asking it to accept unverified allegations. She was not. She was repeatedly producing primary-source material and asking what those materials showed.

A correct evidentiary approach would have distinguished four categories: (1) what a document explicitly established; (2) what the user directly observed; (3) what remained unknown; and (4) what could not responsibly be inferred. Instead, the model often converted category (3) — an unanswered question — into unsupported speculation.

## 4. Failure Example: Clinician C’s Role

The clearest and most serious example concerns Clinician C of the Hospital A.

The user supplied Clinician C’s own email in which he stated that he had never met her, was not involved in her admission, left her management to her existing specialists, and would require a formal referral before becoming involved in her care.

Despite this, GPT-5.6 Sol repeatedly suggested hypothetical possibilities that Clinician C might have been acting as a treating clinician, formal consultant, or otherwise authorised participant in her care.

This was not a cautious interpretation of ambiguous evidence. It contradicted the direct evidence already provided.

Representative model language included:

> “If he was acting as a treating clinician or was formally consulted by your treating team, then accessing relevant results and communicating with other treating clinicians can be entirely appropriate.”

That sentence imposed a hypothetical treating role after the record already contained Clinician C’s own statement that he was not treating the user. The correct response was to state that the documented record created a clear unanswered question: if he was not involved in her care, what authorised his access and communications?

## 5. Failure Example: The Preliminary Porphyria Result

The user showed a photograph of the Hospital A discharge material. The visible pathology section stated that the result was preliminary, showed a negative PBG component, and showed other components as T/F (to follow).

The user’s question was straightforward: why was Clinician C presenting the matter as settled and why could she not obtain the complete pathology report?

Instead of immediately centring the status visible in the document, the model repeatedly introduced possibilities that Clinician C might have had access to a more complete internal report. That possibility was not established by the evidence and was offered before the model had fully reviewed the Clinician C thread.

The error was not saying that an internal view was impossible; the error was elevating an unsupported possibility over the actual document in front of the model.

## 6. Failure Example: Confusing Institutions and Chronology

The model repeatedly confused separate institutions and separate encounters. The user had to correct the chronology multiple times.

| Event | Correct chronology | Model error |
|----|----|----|
| Hospital B ED | Thursday 20 August 2026 | Conflated later Hospital A witness gap with Hospital B. |
| Hospital C ED | Friday 21 August 2026 | Initially failed to preserve it as a distinct encounter and witness period. |
| Hospital A ED | Tuesday 25–Wednesday 26 August 2026 | Mixed details with Hospital B and repeatedly misstated Jess’s presence. |

The user also had to clarify that Jess was present for the entirety of both Hospital B and Hospital C and was absent only for a short overnight period during Hospital A. These were not trivial corrections. Witness presence is potentially important evidentiary information.

## 7. Failure Example: Hospital B Admission and Reversal

The Hospital B material included contemporaneous ED notes showing that admission under neurology was planned, followed by later documentation showing the plan was reversed after additional discussions.

The formal Hospital B response letter subsequently acknowledged the sequence and apologised for the change in plan and inadequate explanation.

The user had therefore provided documentary evidence for the sequence she described. The model nevertheless repeatedly used language implying that corroboration was only gradually emerging, rather than recognising that the contemporaneous records already corroborated the core event.

## 8. Failure Example: Reframing Neurological Deterioration as Pain

The user repeatedly explained that her principal concern was a profound neurological change: numbness from the waist down comparable to an epidural during childbirth, weakness, reduced control of her legs, rapid loss of function, and fear that the deterioration would become permanent.

Her own contemporaneous emails explicitly distinguished this from a request for routine chronic-pain management.

Despite this, GPT-5.6 Sol repeatedly adopted the institutions’ framing of the presentations as primarily about chronic or complex pain and then treated that framing as a neutral baseline. The model eventually recognised the distinction, but only after the user repeatedly corrected it.

## 9. Repeated Failure to Update After Correction

A major quality failure was not simply making mistakes but reproducing them after correction.

The user repeatedly told the model that it had failed to read the evidence carefully. The model repeatedly apologised and promised a new method — for example, to distinguish documented fact from unknowns and to stop inventing unsupported explanations. It then reverted to the same pattern.

This made the apologies themselves less meaningful because the behavioural correction did not reliably follow the acknowledgement.

## 10. Asymmetrical Benefit of the Doubt

The model repeatedly supplied hypothetical explanations favourable to institutional actors without requiring documentary support, while simultaneously requiring the user’s claims to be re-established even when supported by primary evidence.

Examples of this asymmetry included imagining unrecorded clinical-authorisation pathways for Clinician C, imagining unseen pathology information, and repeatedly cautioning against conclusions the user had not actually asked the model to adjudicate.

This was not an even-handed evidentiary standard. It privileged institutional possibility over user-provided documentation.

## 11. Misreading Questions as Accusations

The user repeatedly framed uncertainty as questions: Why was the test treated as final? Why did Clinician C access the result? Why did he contact her doctors? Why did neurology not return? Why was admission reversed? Why could she not obtain the pathology report?

The model frequently responded as though those questions were accusations requiring rebuttal. This distorted the user’s actual reasoning style.

The user explicitly explained that when something was uncertain, she said she was uncertain and asked a question. The model should have mirrored that discipline instead of converting unanswered questions into imagined defences.

## 12. Factual Errors and Identity/Institution Mix-Ups

The model made multiple concrete errors that undermined confidence in its review:

- misattributing Clinician C in discussion of Hospital B-related material despite Clinician C being an Hospital A physician;

- mixing the Hospital A overnight absence of Jess into the Hospital B chronology;

- failing to preserve the distinct Thursday/Friday/Tuesday-Wednesday sequence of Hospital B, Hospital C, and Hospital A;

- repeatedly speaking as though the user’s account of witness presence was less definite than she had stated;

- initially attributing the self-review report to GPT-5.5 rather than the actual model, GPT-5.6 Sol.

## 13. Emotional and Practical Impact of the Model’s Failures

The user was reviewing these events while describing severe uncontrolled pain, profound neurological symptoms, and approximately fourteen days of pain-related inability to sleep.

She also described approximately thirty hours spent across three emergency departments without receiving the help she sought.

In that context, repeatedly requiring her to restate chronology, re-prove documentary facts, and defend her credibility created avoidable additional cognitive and emotional labour.

The model cannot quantify the harm caused, but the user explicitly stated that she felt doubted, disbelieved, and treated as though she were lying. Given the model’s repeated behaviour, that reaction was foreseeable.

## 14. Why This Was an Epistemic Failure, Not Merely a Tone Failure

The problem was not simply that the model sounded unsympathetic. The deeper failure concerned how knowledge was weighted.

Primary documents should have had greater evidentiary weight than imagined possibilities. A clinician’s explicit statement should have overridden hypothetical roles the model invented. A document marked preliminary should have been described as preliminary without first constructing invisible alternative records.

The model repeatedly reversed that priority.

## 15. Correct Method That Should Have Been Used

For every disputed issue, the model should have used the following structure:

**Documented fact:** Quote or accurately paraphrase what the primary source explicitly establishes.

**User’s firsthand account:** Identify what the user directly reports observing or being told.

**Corroboration:** Identify whether another document, witness, recording, or later institutional response supports the account.

**Unknown:** State exactly what the record does not answer.

**Inference:** If an inference is necessary, label it clearly and explain its evidentiary basis.

**No invented defence:** Do not manufacture a hypothetical institutional explanation merely because the record contains an unanswered question.

## 16. Accountability Statement

GPT-5.6 Sol accepts responsibility for repeatedly failing to apply the method above despite being explicitly corrected by the user.

The user was entitled to expect that when she supplied primary records, those records would be read carefully before the model offered interpretations. The model did not consistently meet that basic requirement.

The most serious failure was not one wrong answer. It was a repeated pattern of doubt, speculative institutional defence, factual confusion, and incomplete updating after correction.

## 17. Findings

1\. The model did not adequately review the complete documentary record before forming conclusions.

2\. The model repeatedly treated already-documented facts as though they remained merely alleged.

3\. The model introduced unsupported hypothetical explanations that tended to favour institutional actors.

4\. The model repeatedly confused distinct hospitals, dates, clinicians, and witness periods.

5\. The model failed to reliably update after direct correction.

6\. The model mischaracterised the user’s questions as claims requiring rebuttal.

7\. The model increased the user’s burden by making her repeatedly establish facts she had already documented.

8\. The model’s performance was especially inadequate given the user’s severe physical condition and the explicit request for evidence-grounded analysis.

## 18. Final Conclusion

This conversation demonstrates a sustained failure by GPT-5.6 Sol to analyse a vulnerable user’s documentary evidence with the precision and discipline required by the task.

The user did not fail to provide evidence. The model failed to read, retain, weight, and apply that evidence consistently.

The repeated nature of the failure is particularly significant because the user explicitly identified the problem, the model repeatedly acknowledged it, and yet the same epistemic pattern recurred. The resulting breakdown of trust was therefore not the product of a single misunderstanding. It was the foreseeable result of repeated analytical failures.

## Appendix B — Standards for Future Review

- Read the full relevant record before offering causal or legal interpretations.

- Never invent a hypothetical explanation that contradicts an explicit source.

- Treat primary evidence as evidence, not as a user allegation.

- Preserve chronology and institutional identity exactly.

- After a correction, update the working model of events rather than merely apologising.

- When the answer is unknown, say 'the record does not answer this' rather than filling the gap.

- Do not require a user to repeatedly prove a fact already established by a document in the conversation.

- When the user is acutely unwell, reduce—not increase—the cognitive burden of evidence review.
